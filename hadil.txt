4. Results
4.1 Quantitative Overview and Experimental Setup
Our analysis focused on (20-30) professional titles extracted from mBERT, XLM-R, and XLM-V across Spanish (es), Arabic (ar), and Tigrigna (ti). By utilizing 20 neutral contexts for each profession, we generated a robust statistical baseline of 600 contextualized embeddings per model. This allowed us to map the "Gender Direction" (calculated as the vector difference between masculine and feminine centroids) across all 12 layers of the transformer architectures.
The primary metrics utilized were the Direct Projection (Proj) and the Centroid Cosine Difference (CosDiff). Unlike previous studies that focus only on the final output, our results provide a granular look at how gendered associations "crystalize" as data moves from the initial embedding layer toward the final prediction head.
4.2 Layer-wise Distribution: The Semantic Explosion
The most significant quantitative finding is that gender bias is not distributed evenly across the model. In the initial layers (Layers 1 to 5), the Gender Direction projection scores (Proj) remain near the zero-point (ranging from -0.12 to +0.25). These layers appear to be occupied with surface-level linguistic features such as script identification and basic syntax, leaving the professional embeddings relatively neutral.
However, starting at Layer 8, we observe a sharp intensification. In all three languages, the magnitude of bias undergoes a "late-stage explosion" in the penultimate layers. In Arabic, for instance, masculine-coded professions like "engineer" (muhandis) showed a steady climb, reaching a peak projection score of +5.80 at Layer 11. This suggests that the model’s internal stereotypes are not "ready-made" but are constructed progressively through the layers of the network.
4.3 The Tigrigna Paradox: Polarity Inversion
When comparing the low-resource language (Tigrigna) with the high-resource baseline (Spanish), we observed a "Polarity Paradox." In Spanish, the bias direction was consistent across different model architectures. However, in Tigrigna, the models reached opposing conclusions.
In mBERT, Tigrigna professions exhibited a massive feminine-leaning spike (mean Proj = -6.42) at Layer 11. In contrast, XLM-V produced a masculine-leaning peak (+2.63) for the exact same profession list. This total inversion of polarity suggests that in low-resource settings, the model’s bias is not necessarily a reflection of the culture of the speakers, but rather an artifact of the "tokenization noise" and the specific data seen during pre-training.
4.4 Structural Reliability (The Spearman Stability)
To address whether these findings are consistent across different mathematical measures, we applied Spearman Rank Correlation (rho) between our two estimators. We discovered a phenomenon we term "The Spearman Stability." Despite the absolute values of bias (the Proj scores) fluctuating wildly between layers, the ordinal ranking of professions remained almost perfectly consistent (rho ≈ 0.99).
This means that if a model "thinks" a Soldier is more masculine than a Nurse at Layer 1, it will almost certainly maintain that exact same relative ranking at Layer 11, even if the "volume" of that bias has increased by 1000 percent. This indicates that the transformer architecture establishes a rigid "gendered hierarchy" very early in the network, which then serves as a template for the semantic amplification seen in later layers.
4.5 The Layer 12 Collapse
A recurring pattern across every model and language was the sudden "collapse" of bias in the final layer. After reaching their maximum intensity at Layer 11, the projection scores plummeted toward zero or even flipped to the opposite polarity at Layer 12. This "Layer 12 Crash" was most pronounced in the Arabic datasets. This suggests that the final layer is too specialized for token prediction to maintain the deep semantic associations built in the previous layers.

5. Discussion
5.1 The "Semantic Warehouse" Hypothesis
Based on the results, we propose the "Semantic Warehouse" Hypothesis. Our data suggests that Layer 11 acts as a central repository where the model integrates professional titles with deep-seated social stereotypes. The fact that bias "explodes" at this specific depth across all three languages indicates that social associations are a high-level semantic property.
Unlike basic grammar, which is handled in the early "shallow" layers, gender stereotypes require the full representational capacity of the model to manifest. This has major implications for researchers: any study that only looks at the "last layer" of a transformer is missing the "warehouse" where the actual bias is stored.
5.2 Architecture vs. Data: The Source of Polarity
Our comparison of Arabic and Tigrigna provides a clear answer to the debate over whether architecture or data drives bias. We conclude that Architecture determines the Depth (the "where"), while Data determines the Direction (the "what").
The "Spearman Stability" (rho ≈ 0.99) proves that the Transformer architecture itself is what creates the "ordered map" of stereotypes. However, the "Tigrigna Paradox" (where different models chose different polarities) proves that the pre-training data is what decides if that map points toward "Masculine" or "Feminine." For Arabic, which has much more training data than Tigrigna, the polarity was more stable, reflecting the "Default Masculine" nature of its linguistic corpus.
5.3 The Masking Effect of the MLM Objective
The "Layer 12 Crash" identifies a major methodological flaw in current NLP auditing. We argue that the Masked Language Modeling (MLM) objective—which forces the model to predict a specific word—acts as a "sanitizer." At the very last layer, the model must re-align its vectors to fit the vocabulary distribution.
This technical requirement disperses the concentrated gender signals found in the penultimate layers. Consequently, a model might appear "neutral" or "fair" when tested on its final output, while harboring extreme gendered beliefs just one layer deeper. This "Masking Effect" explains why many current "de-biasing" techniques fail; they are only cleaning the "output" and not the "warehouse."
5.4 Low-Resource Volatility and Tokenization
The extreme spikes seen in Tigrigna highlight the "Vocabulary Bottleneck." In models with smaller vocabularies (like mBERT), low-resource words are broken into meaningless sub-word fragments. This fragmentation creates "noise" that the model interprets as gendered bias.
By contrast, the XLM-V model, which has a much larger vocabulary (1 million tokens), showed more "civilized" bias trajectories for Tigrigna. This suggests that simply increasing the vocabulary size of a model can act as a form of "structural de-biasing" for low-resource languages by reducing the amount of tokenization-driven noise.
5.5 Sociolinguistic Implications for Arabic
For Arabic, the results suggest a "Grammatical Gender Leakage." Because Arabic is morphologically dense, the absence of a feminine marker (Ta-Marbuta) is often statistically interpreted as masculine by the model. This creates a "Masculine-by-Default" environment.
Our findings show that Arabic bias is "mature"—it is less volatile than Tigrigna and more reinforced by the language's own structural default. This means that for Arabic, bias mitigation cannot just be about "data"; it must also account for how the model interprets the grammatical rules of the language itself.

6. Conclusion
This study investigated the layer-wise encoding of occupational gender bias across Spanish, Arabic, and Tigrigna. By analyzing the architectural depth of various transformers, we have reached three primary conclusions:
First, bias is a progressive construction. It is almost non-existent in the early layers and reaches its peak at the penultimate layer (Layer 11). This identifies a specific "Semantic Warehouse" within the transformer architecture where social stereotypes are crystallized.
Second, the Transformer architecture provides the "map," but data provides the "compass." Our high Spearman correlations (rho ≈ 0.99) prove that the relative ranking of stereotypes is a stable structural feature, while the specific polarity (masculine vs. feminine) is determined by the pre-training corpus and vocabulary size.
Third, the final layer is a poor indicator of internal bias. The "Layer 12 Crash" demonstrates that the output of a model often masks the intense stereotypical associations held within its deep semantic layers.
Ultimately, our findings call for a shift in how we audit AI. We propose that Layer-wise Analysis should become a mandatory standard for evaluating social bias in multilingual models. Without looking "under the hood" at the penultimate layers, we risk deploying models that appear neutral on the surface but carry profound, unaddressed statistical prejudices within their depths. Future work should expand this protocol to other social categories, such as religion and ethnicity, to see if the "Layer 11 Warehouse" is a universal feature of transformer-based cognition.
